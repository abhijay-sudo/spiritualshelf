A boat full of leaves

A merchant promises a vow if he is given a child, then forgets it. Prison follows, then a cargo turned to leaves, then a boat that sinks within reach of home.

Satyanarayan Katha. Also known as: Satyanarayan Vrat Katha, Story of Sadhu the merchant.

  • Truth
  • Devotion
हिन्दी

In the Naimisha forest, Shaunaka and the asked Suta: what vow or penance wins the heart’s wish? Suta retold the Lord’s answer to Narada. The katha names the Reva Khanda of the Skanda as its home, though not every edition carries it.

Roaming the worlds to help others, Narada came among mortals. Everywhere they suffered, worn down by their own deeds. He went to Vishnu: shining white, four-armed, with conch, discus, mace and lotus.

“Lord, is there a simple way to end their suffering?”

“There is a vow rare in heaven and on earth. On any day, at nightfall, with brahmanas and kin, worship Satyanarayana. Offer bananas, ghee, milk and wheat flour, a quarter over the measure. Hear the katha. Eat the prasad.”

In Kashi, the poor brahmana Shatananda begged daily, sick with hunger and thirst. An old brahmana asked courteously, “Why do you roam so unhappy?”

“I am poor. If you know a way, tell me.”

The old man, Satyanarayana himself, taught him the vow and vanished. Next day the alms poured in. Shatananda kept it with his kin, and every month after.

One day, as he began, a thirsty woodseller left his load outside and came in. “What does this vow bring?”

“Every wish. All I have is by his grace.”

The woodseller drank, ate the prasad, and sold his wood where the rich lived. It fetched double. He kept the vow. Wealth came, and sons.

Then Suta told of a king, and a merchant’s promise.

On the bank of the river Bhadrashila, King Ulkamukha was keeping the vow with his lotus-faced queen. Wise and truthful, he gave the brahmanas wealth every day. A merchant named Sadhu, out trading with great wealth, moored his boat there and came up.

“King, what is this worship?”

“Of Vishnu, with my kin, for sons.”

“I have no child either. Teach me, and I will do it.”

Home again, Sadhu told his wife Lilavati, “I will keep the vow when we have a child.”

By the Lord’s grace, in the tenth month, a daughter was born, growing day by day like the waxing moon. They named her Kalavati.

“Why have you not kept the vow you promised?” Lilavati asked gently.

“At her wedding, my love.”

Kalavati grew up. Sadhu’s messenger went to the city of Kanchana and brought back a merchant’s son, handsome and good. Sadhu gave him his daughter with every . As fate would have it, at the wedding he forgot the vow. The Lord was angered.

In time Sadhu went with his son-in-law to trade at Ratnasarapura by the sea, King Chandraketu’s city. There Satyanarayana saw the man who had broken his word, and cursed him: “Hard, terrible sorrow will be his.”

One day a thief stole the king’s treasure. Seeing guards racing after him, he left it where the two merchants were and slipped away.

The guards bound them both. “My lord, we have brought two thieves!”

Without inquiry, both were chained in a great fort.

By the Lord’s maya, no one heard a word they said. King Chandraketu took their wealth too.

At home, a thief carried off everything. Lilavati, sick in body and mind, hungry and thirsty, went from door to door for food. Kalavati wandered too.

One day, hungry, Kalavati came to a brahmana’s house where the vow was being kept. She sat, heard the katha, prayed for a boon, ate the prasad, and went home at night.

“Daughter, where were you tonight? What is on your mind?”

“Mother, in a brahmana’s house I saw a vow that grants wishes.”

Lilavati kept it with her kin. “Let my husband and son-in-law come home soon. Forgive them their offence.”

Pleased, the Lord came to King Chandraketu in a dream.

“King, free the two merchants at dawn. Give back all you took. Or I will destroy you, with your kingdom, your wealth and your sons.”

At dawn the merchants were brought in, freed of their fetters, too frightened to speak. “Fate brought you great sorrow,” said Chandraketu. “Now there is nothing to fear.” He gave them clothes and ornaments, returned their wealth twice over and sent them home.

Sadhu gave gifts to the brahmanas and set out. A little way along, an ascetic with a staff asked, “Sadhu, what is in your boat?”

The two merchants, swollen with pride, laughed at him. “Why ask, ascetic? After our coins? There is nothing aboard but creepers and leaves.”

“Then let your words be true.”

The ascetic walked away and stood by the sea. Sadhu finished his daily rites, and stared. His boat was riding high. Inside lay creepers and leaves. He fell senseless.

“That ascetic can do anything,” said his son-in-law. “Let us go to him for refuge.”

Sadhu bowed to him again and again, weeping. “Forgive what I said.”

“Do not weep. Listen. You turned from my worship, fool. By my will you suffered, again and again.”

“Even Brahma and the gods are bewildered by your maya. How could I know you? Be gracious. I will worship you with all I have.”

The Lord granted his wish and vanished. The boat was full of wealth again. Sadhu worshipped with his people and sailed for home.

Near his city, Sadhu sent a messenger ahead. Lilavati, overjoyed, finished her worship and called, “Come quickly, see your father!” Kalavati finished the vow, left the prasad untouched, and hurried to her husband.

Angered, the Lord sank the boat, husband, wealth and all.

Not seeing her husband anywhere, Kalavati fell to the ground, weeping. “Who can know the greatness of Satyadeva?” cried Lilavati, and drew her into her lap. Kalavati took up her husband’s sandals. She meant to follow him.

“Satyadeva has taken him,” thought Sadhu, and lay flat before the Lord again and again.

A voice came from the sky. “Your daughter left the prasad to see her husband. Let her go home, eat it and come back, and she will have him again.”

Kalavati hurried home, ate the prasad, and came back. There stood her husband among their people. “Father, come home. Why do you wait?” From then on, Sadhu kept the vow at full moon and sankranti.

Then Suta told of King Tungadhvaja, who cared for his people. After hunting in the forest, he came to a banyan where cowherds and their kin were worshipping Satyadeva. Pride kept the king away. He did not bow. They set the prasad beside him and went back to eat. He left it lying there.

His hundred sons were lost, his wealth gone. “Satyadeva has done this. I will go where he is worshipped.” He worshipped with the cowherds, in devotion and faith. By Satyadeva’s grace he had wealth and sons.

Word meanings & sources

  1. sage ऋषि ṛṣi

    Literal“Seer.” One who perceives; the Vedic word for a poet-visionary who hears rather than invents.

    MeaningA rishi: a seer, teacher or ascetic living in the forest.

  2. Purana

    Meaning“Ancient.” A body of texts holding the stories of the gods, creation and kings.

  3. rite यज्ञ yajña

    LiteralFrom yaj, to worship, to offer. The fire-rite of the Vedas; later, any disciplined act given away.

    MeaningA sacred offering made with fire and mantra; also any act performed as an offering.
