A king shelters a tiny fish from the river. It outgrows a waterpot, a well, a lake. Then it tells him: on the seventh day the three worlds will drown.

“Tell me of Hari’s first ,” King Parikshit said, “the fish. Why would the Lord, like one bound by karma, wear a form the world thinks lowly, dark-natured, hard to bear?”

Shuka, Vyasa’s son, answered. “To guard cows, brahmanas, gods, the good, the Vedas, and artha, the Lord takes bodies. Like the wind he moves among creatures high and low, and is himself neither.”

Then he began.

The past kalpa was ending. ’s day was done, and the sea had covered the three worlds, from the earth upward. Sleep came upon the Creator. As he wished to lie down, the Vedas slipped from his mouth.

Close by was mighty Hayagriva, lord of the Danavas. He carried them off.

Hari knew what Hayagriva had done. He took the form of a tiny fish.

There was a royal then: , lord of the Dravida country, devoted to Narayana, living on water alone for his tapas.

One day, at the river Kritamala, he was offering water. In the water cupped in his hands, a tiny fish appeared. He let it go into the river with the water.

The fish cried out pitifully. “King, kind to the helpless! I am afraid. How can you leave me in this river, among water-creatures that kill their own kind?”

He did not know who was asking. Moved, he set it in the water of his waterpot and carried it home to his hermitage.

In a single night the fish outgrew the waterpot.

“I cannot live cramped in this waterpot. Find me a roomier home.”

He put it in a well. In a moment it had grown three cubits.

“This will not do, King. Give me a wide place. I have come to you for shelter.”

He carried it to a lake. The great fish covered the lake and went on growing.

“I am a creature of water, and this is no good to me. Put me in a lake that never shrinks.”

He took it from one unfailing lake to the next. At last he cast it into the ocean.

“Hero, the makaras here are mighty. They will eat me. Do not leave me here.”

The sweet words bewildered him. Then Satyavrata spoke.

“Who are you, who bewilder us in a fish’s shape? We have never seen or heard of a water-creature of such power. In a single day you filled a lake of a hundred yojanas.

“Surely you are the Lord himself, Hari, Narayana, the imperishable, wearing this form out of kindness to the living. I bow to you, lord of the world’s making, keeping and ending. For us who have given ourselves to you, you are the highest refuge.

“All your descents are for the good of creatures. Why have you taken this one? Lotus-eyed one, no one comes to your feet in vain.”

The fish answered.

The Lord of the world, in a fish’s body, wished to play in the waters of dissolution, and to do good to one who loved him. He spoke.

“On the seventh day from today, tamer of foes, the three worlds will sink. As they go under, a great boat sent by me will come to you.

“Take every herb, and seeds great and small. With the seven around you and every kind of creature beside you, board it. You will move without fear over the one ocean, lit only by the sages’ radiance.

“When a mighty wind tosses the boat, I will be near. Bind it to my horn with the great serpent. Until Brahma’s night ends, I will draw you on.”

Hari vanished.

Satyavrata waited for the hour he had been told of. He spread darbha grass, tips to the east, sat facing north-east, and fixed his mind on the feet of Hari, the fish.

Then great clouds poured. The sea swelled over its shores and flooded the earth on every side.

Remembering the Lord’s command, he saw it: the boat had come. He boarded it with the great brahmanas, carrying herbs and creepers.

“King,” the said, “fix your mind on Keshava. He will bring us through.”

He did. In the great ocean a fish appeared: golden, single-horned, a niyuta of yojanas long.

As Hari had told him, the king bound the boat to that horn, with the serpent for a rope.

Glad at heart, the king praised Madhusudana.

“An ignorance without beginning has covered our knowledge of ourselves, and its toil has worn us out. You set us free. You are our highest guru.

“As gold and silver shed their dross in fire, one who serves you sheds his darkness and takes back his own colour. Be our guru, guru of gurus.

“The unknowing, taught by the unknowing, are the blind led by the blind. You see like the sun. You live in the heart, and the world, blind with wanting, does not know you.

“With words that are lamps, cut the knots in my heart. Show me my own true home.”

Moving through the great ocean in a fish’s form, the first Person taught the king: a divine gathering of ancient lore, with sankhya, yoga and the rites, and the whole secret of the self. Seated in the boat with the , Satyavrata heard the eternal Brahman from the Lord himself. No doubt was left.

The dissolution passed, and Brahma rose. Hari slew the asura Hayagriva and gave the Vedas back to the Creator.

And Satyavrata, by ’s grace, became the Manu of this kalpa: Vaivasvata Manu, son of Vivasvan, known as Shraddhadeva.

Shuka bowed. “To the cause of all, who played the fish, brought back the Vedas taken from the sleeping Creator, and taught Brahman to Satyavrata and the : to him I bow.”

Original retellings, not scripture text or translations.

  1. Bhagavata Purana 8.24.1-58 and 8.24.61 (Parikshit’s question and Shuka’s answer; Hayagriva carries off the Vedas as Brahma grows sleepy; King Satyavrata and the tiny fish at the river Kritamala; the pot, the well, the lake and the ocean; the king knows the fish for Hari; the promise of the seventh day; the flood, the boat and the golden fish with one horn; the king’s prayer; the Lord’s teaching; Hayagriva slain and the Vedas returned to Brahma; Satyavrata as Vaivasvata Manu; Shuka’s closing bow) - Sanskrit with word-for-word meanings
  2. An older telling, not the Bhagavata and not used in this story: Shatapatha Brahmana 1.8.1.1-6, in Julius Eggeling’s translation (a fish comes into Manu’s hands as he washes and asks to be reared; a jar, a pit, then the sea; a flood foretold; Manu ties the rope of his ship to the fish’s horn and is brought to the northern mountain; no god is named as the fish)
  3. A different telling, not the Bhagavata and not used in this story: Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section 186 in K. M. Ganguli’s translation (Markandeya tells Yudhishthira of Vaivasvata Manu, who is doing penance when a little fish at the river Chirini asks his protection from the larger fish; an earthen vessel, a tank, the Ganga, then the sea; the ark, the seven rishis and the seeds; the ark tied to a peak of the Himavat; at the end the fish says he is Brahma)
