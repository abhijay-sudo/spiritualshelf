Akrura and the fall of Kamsa. Also known as: Kamsa Vadh, Krishna leaves Vraja.

In Hindi: अक्रूर और कंस का अंत, कंस वध, कृष्ण का मथुरा गमन.

Arishta the bull demon lay dead in , killed by . Then the Narada came to .

“’s child was a daughter,” he said. “Krishna is ’s son, and Rama is Rohini’s. , afraid, left both boys with his friend . They are the two who killed your men.”

Kamsa went wild. He snatched up a sharp sword to kill Vasudeva. Narada stopped him: Vasudeva’s sons, not Vasudeva, would be his death. So Kamsa had Vasudeva and Devaki chained in iron once more.

Narada left. Kamsa called Keshi. “Go. Kill Rama and Krishna.”

Then he sent for Chanura and Mushtika.

“Rama and Krishna, Vasudeva’s sons, live in Nanda’s village,” Kamsa told Chanura and Mushtika. “It is foretold they will be my death. When they are brought here, kill them under cover of a wrestling match.”

He ordered a ring built, the whole city called to watch. The elephant Kuvalayapida would stand at the gate. The bow festival would open on Chaturdashi.

Then he took Akrura’s hand. “No one has been kinder to us than you. Go to Nanda’s village. Bring the two sons of Vasudeva here in this chariot, and tell Nanda to bring tribute.”

“You have planned well,” said Akrura. “But destiny decides how plans end. Still, I will do as you command.”

After a night in Mathura, he drove to .

All the way, Akrura could hardly believe it. What good deed had he ever done, that today he would see Keshava?

“Kamsa sent me,” he thought, “and yet it is Kamsa who gives me this.” The deer crossed on his right. “Surely I will see Mukunda’s face. When I bow at his feet, he will not count me an enemy. He sees into every heart.”

The sun was setting when he reached Gokula. In the pasture lay footprints marked with the lotus, the barleycorn and the elephant goad. Akrura leapt from the chariot and rolled in the dust, weeping. “The dust of my master’s feet!”

Then he saw them, going out to milk the cows: Krishna in yellow, in blue, one dark, one fair.

Akrura fell at their feet, flat as a rod. Krishna drew him close and embraced him. Balarama took his joined hands and led him home: milk with honey, the gift of a cow, supper.

Nanda spoke. “How do you live while Kamsa lives? You are sheep in a butcher’s hands. He killed his own sister’s babies while she cried out.”

After the meal Krishna asked what the king meant to do. “Kamsa, whom all call our uncle, is a blight on our family! For my sake my parents lost their sons and were jailed!”

Akrura told it all: Kamsa’s hatred, Narada’s news, why he had come. Krishna and Balarama laughed.

Nanda’s order ran through : gather milk and ghee, yoke the wagons. Tomorrow, Mathura.

When the young women of heard, some turned pale. Some let bracelets and braids slip loose. Some fainted, remembering his words.

“O Fate, how hard you are,” they said. “You join friends, then pull them apart, as in a child’s game. You showed us his face; now you cover it. He is named Akrura, yet he is cruel. He takes Krishna, our life, and gives us no kind word. The elders stay silent. Let us stop him ourselves!”

Forgetting shame, they cried: “Govinda! Damodara! Madhava!”

At sunrise Akrura drove. Nanda and the cowherds followed in wagons loaded with pots of ghee. Krishna sent a messenger back: “I will return.”

They stood like painted figures until the chariot’s flag was gone, then its dust.

At the Yamuna, Krishna touched the water and drank. Akrura went to bathe. Reciting mantras as he bathed, he suddenly saw Balarama and Krishna there in the water.

He climbed out. They sat in the chariot as before. He went under again.

Now he saw Shesha, thousand-headed, white as , honoured by Siddhas and Gandharvas. On his lap lay a Person dark as a rain cloud, in yellow cloth, four-armed, with lotus, conch, disc and club. Brahma, Rudra, Prahlada and Narada stood round him.

Akrura bowed. “I bow to Narayana, from whose navel-lotus Brahma was born. Protect me.”

The vision vanished. “Did you see something wonderful?” Krishna asked.

“All wonders are in you,” said Akrura. “Having seen you, what is left to see?”

Nanda’s carts waited in a garden outside Mathura. Krishna sent Akrura home: first, he said, he must kill the enemy of the Yadus. Heavy-hearted, Akrura told Kamsa they had come, and went home.

At evening the boys passed through the crystal gates. Women crowded the rooftops, one with an earring on one ear only, flowers raining down.

Krishna asked a washerman, the king’s servant, for fine clothes. The man answered with insults, and Krishna killed him. A weaver decorated their clothes; Sudama the garland-maker hung them with flowers. Trivakra, Kamsa’s maidservant, gave them ointment, and at Krishna’s touch her bent back straightened. (Later tellings call her Kubja.)

At the festival ground he lifted the great bow, strung it, and snapped it like sugarcane.

The bow’s crack rang out in every direction, and Kamsa shook with fear. The guards who rushed Krishna fell, and so did the soldiers sent after them. The boys ate rice and milk at the camp and rested there comfortably.

Kamsa did not sleep. In his reflection he had no head. The moon showed double, his shadow had a hole in it, and he could not hear his own breath. Dreaming, he rode a donkey and drank poison.

At dawn the drums sounded. At the arena gate stood Kuvalayapida, goaded by his keeper. “Move,” said Krishna, “or I send you both to Yama.” The elephant charged.

When it was over, Krishna walked into the arena with a tusk on his shoulder, and Balarama beside him.

“The king wants to see you wrestle,” said Chanura.

“We are forest folk,” said Krishna, “but we are the king’s subjects.”

“You are no mere boys,” said Chanura. “Fight me, Krishna. Balarama, take Mushtika.”

The women cried shame: grown wrestlers against tender boys. Devaki and Vasudeva grieved, not knowing their sons’ strength.

Chanura fell to Krishna, Mushtika to Balarama. The others fell or fled.

All cheered but Kamsa. “Drive them out! Seize Nanda! Kill Vasudeva, and my father Ugrasena!” Krishna leapt onto the dais. Kamsa rose with sword and shield. Krishna seized him by the hair, threw him down, and Kamsa died there. His eight brothers rushed in; Balarama met them.

The widows came weeping, saying his violence against the innocent had brought him here.

Krishna consoled the widows and had the funeral rites done. Then he freed his mother and father and bowed, head at their feet.

Devaki and Vasudeva now knew who he was. They stood with folded hands and did not embrace him. He spread his Yogamaya over them, and he was their son again. “You worried for us all those years,” he said, “and could never enjoy our childhood. Please forgive us.” They lifted him into their laps and wept.

He made his mother’s father, Ugrasena, king of the Yadus.

Then he went to Nanda. “Father, you and Yashoda cared for us as your own. Go home to . We will come to you.”

Nanda wept, embraced them both, and turned the cowherds back toward .
