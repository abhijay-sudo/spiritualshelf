Two boys in bark sing Rama’s own story at his sacrifice and refuse his gold. Then he sends for their mother.

Lava, Kusha and Sita. Also known as: Luv Kush, Sita enters the earth, Sita’s bhumi pravesh.

Rama sat with his companions. Mid-talk, he spoke.

“Bhadra, what do the townspeople say of me? Of Sita, of my brothers? Hide nothing.”

Bhadra joined his palms. “Hear, king, what they say in squares and markets, in streets and gardens. Rama bridged the sea, which not even the gods had done. He killed Ravana. And then they say: Ravana once took Sita by force, Lanka kept her in its Ashoka grove, in the rakshasas’ power, and still Rama took her home. Now we too shall have to bear such things from our wives. As the king does, the people follow.”

Rama asked the others. They bowed their heads to the ground. “It is so.”

Rama sent them away. Then he sent for his brothers.

Bharata, Lakshmana and Shatrughna found his face like the moon in eclipse, his eyes full of tears.

“Lakshmana, you saw Sita enter the fire at Lanka,” Rama said. “Fire and wind, sun and moon declared her sinless before the gods and . My heart knows that she is pure. But the people’s talk has pierced me. For fear of a bad name I would give up my life, even you, my brothers.

“Tomorrow at dawn, take Sita in Sumantra’s chariot, across the Ganga, to Valmiki’s hermitage on the Tamasa. Leave her there. Do not answer me. She asked to see the hermitages by the Ganga. Let her wish be granted.”

Then, his face wet with tears, he went in, sighing like an elephant.

At dawn Lakshmana told Sita her wish was granted. Glad, she gathered fine clothes and jewels for the hermits’ wives.

On the road she said, “My eye throbs. My body trembles. The earth looks empty to me. Is all well with your brother?”

“All is well,” said Lakshmana.

Next day at midday, beside the Ganga, he broke down and wept aloud.

“Why weep now, when my wish is coming true?” said Sita. “Take me across.”

They crossed in the Nishadas’ wide boat. On the far bank Lakshmana joined his palms, tears streaming.

“The king heard the people’s talk. He has given you up, though you were proved pure before me. Here is Valmiki’s hermitage. He was our father’s dear friend. Do not grieve.”

Sita fell to the ground. When she could speak, she said, “This body was made for sorrow. I would give up my life in the Ganga, but then the king’s line would end with me.

“Bow to my mothers-in-law for me. And tell the king: you know truly that Sita is pure. Treat the citizens as you treat your brothers. I do not grieve for myself. A husband is a woman’s god.”

Lakshmana walked around her, weeping. “I have never looked at more of you than your feet,” he said. “How can I look at you now, alone, without Rama?”

He crossed back, turning again and again. She watched the chariot go far away.

Then, in a forest loud with peacocks, Sita wept aloud.

The hermit boys saw her weeping and ran to Valmiki. “A woman like Shri herself is weeping by the river!”

Valmiki came to the bank with water of welcome. “Daughter-in-law of Dasharatha, daughter of Janaka, welcome. By the eye of my tapas I know you are sinless. The hermit women will cherish you like their own child.” She touched his feet with her head and followed him.

On the night Shatrughna, on his way to the Yamuna, stayed in a leaf hut there, Sita bore twin sons. Valmiki took a fistful of kusha grass and its lower part, called lava. “Rub the elder with kusha, the younger with lava. Kusha and Lava they shall be.”

At midnight Shatrughna heard. “Mother, what good fortune!”

The boys grew, and learned Valmiki’s whole poem by heart.

Rama held a horse sacrifice in the Naimisha forest by the Gomati, with a golden image of his wife for the . Valmiki came with his disciples and built huts nearby. He called Sita’s sons.

“Sing the whole Ramayana, in the hermitages, in the streets, at Rama’s door, before the priests. Eat these fruits from the hilltops and you will not tire. Twenty sargas a day. Take nothing. What is wealth to us, who live on fruits and roots? If the king asks whose sons you are, say: Valmiki’s disciples. Do not slight him. By , the king is father of all.”

At dawn the brothers bathed, fed the fire, tuned their strings, and sang.

Between the rites, Rama gathered , kings, scholars and musicians to hear them.

The crowd drank the boys in with their eyes. “Rama’s image, one reflection from another. But for the matted hair and bark, you could not tell them from him.”

They sang twenty sargas, from the first, the one Narada had shown. In the afternoon Rama told his brother, “Give them eighteen thousand pieces of gold.”

They would not take it. “We live in the forest on roots and fruits. What would we do with gold?”

“Who made this poem?” asked Rama.

“Valmiki, who has come to your sacrifice. Twenty-four thousand verses. Hear it all, if you wish.”

Day after day Rama listened. In the song he understood whose sons they were.

He sent messengers to Valmiki. “If Sita is sinless, let her, with the ’s leave, take an oath before the assembly at dawn, to clear herself and me.”

“So be it,” said Valmiki.

At dawn they gathered, Vasishtha and Vishvamitra and many , kings, , rakshasas, thousands from every land, still as stone. Valmiki walked in. Behind him came Sita, head bowed, palms joined, throat thick with tears, like the Veda following Brahma.

“These twins are your sons,” said Valmiki. “I am Pracheta’s tenth son, and I do not remember ever speaking a lie. For many thousands of years I have done tapas. If she is at fault, may I never taste its fruit.”

“I believe you,” said Rama. “I knew she was sinless when I gave her up. Forgive me. I know these twins are my sons.”

A pure, fragrant wind blew. Sita, in ochre cloth, eyes on the ground, spoke.

“As I have never thought of anyone but Rama, may the goddess Earth open for me. As I honour Rama in mind, deed and word, may the goddess Earth open for me. As this is true, and I know none but Rama, may the goddess Earth open for me.”

A throne rose from the ground, borne on the heads of nagas. The Earth goddess took Sita in her arms, welcomed her and seated her. As Sita sank into the deep, flowers fell without end.

Rama leaned on his staff, head bowed, and wept a long time. Then grief turned to anger.

“Before my eyes she is gone. Once I brought her back from beyond the ocean. Why not from inside the earth? Goddess Earth, you are my mother-in-law: Janaka lifted her out of you with his plough. Give her back, or I will shatter you, mountains, forests and all, and let everything be water.”

Brahma spoke. “Rama, do not grieve. Remember who you were before: Vishnu. Sita, the pure, has gone in happiness to the realm of the nagas, by the power of her devotion to you. You will meet again in heaven.”

Rama took Kusha and Lava to his leaf hut. All night he mourned for Sita.

Word meanings & sources

Tap any underlined word for its meaning, what it means in this story, and where it comes from.

  1. sage ऋषि ṛṣi

    Literal“Seer.” One who perceives; the Vedic word for a poet-visionary who hears rather than invents.

    MeaningA rishi: a seer, teacher or ascetic living in the forest.

  2. rite यज्ञ yajña

    LiteralFrom yaj, to worship, to offer. The fire-rite of the Vedas; later, any disciplined act given away.

    MeaningA sacrifice: offerings made into a sacred fire with mantras, for a god or for a purpose; also any act performed as an offering.

  3. dharma धर्म dharma

    MeaningWhat upholds: duty, order and right action, as the situation asks.

  4. vanara वानर vānara

    MeaningThe forest people of Kishkindha, Hanuman’s people.
