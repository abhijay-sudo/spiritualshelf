A newborn sees the rising sun, takes it for a fruit, and leaps. Years later, on a hopeless shore, old Jambavan reminds him who he is.

Hanuman and the sun. Also known as: Bal Hanuman and Surya, Birth of Hanuman.

  • Fearlessness
  • Hope
हिन्दी

A hundred yojanas of ocean lay before them. At Angada’s word, each chief said how far he could leap.

Gaja, ten yojanas. Gavaksha, twenty. Gavaya, thirty. Sharabha, forty. Gandhamadana, fifty. Mainda, sixty. Dvivida, seventy. Sushena, eighty.

Then Jambavan, eldest of them all. “Ninety. Once it was more: at Bali’s sacrifice I walked around Vishnu as he took his three strides. Now I am old. Ninety is not enough.”

“I can go the hundred,” said Angada. “Whether I can come back, I cannot say.”

“A master sends; he is not sent,” said Jambavan.

“Then, if no one goes, we fast to death again.”

“This will not fail,” said Jambavan. “I will rouse the one who can do it.”

Apart from them all, alone, sat Hanuman.

Jambavan saw the army, hundreds of thousands, in despair, and turned to Hanuman.

“Why sit alone and silent, you who know every shastra? In strength and fire you equal Sugriva, Rama and Lakshmana. Why do you not know yourself?”

Then he told Hanuman who he was.

His mother Anjana, Kesari’s wife, was once Punjikasthala, finest of apsaras, cursed to be born a vanari. In human form, garlanded, in yellow silk, she stood on a mountaintop. The Wind saw her, loved her, and took her in his long arms.

“Who would break my vow to my husband?” she cried.

“I am not hurting you,” said the Wind. “I came to you in mind alone. You will have a son, mighty and wise, my equal in leaping.”

A newborn lay crying in the forest, alone and hungry. His mother had gone to gather fruit.

So the Agastya would tell it to Rama, after the war, in Hanuman’s presence.

Then the child saw the sun rising, red as a heap of hibiscus. A fruit! He sprang into the sky, a small sun leaping at the young sun.

Gods, danavas and yakshas marvelled. “Not the Wind, not Garuda, not even thought is this fast!”

His father the Wind flew after him, cool as snow, so the sun would not burn him. Thousands of yojanas the child climbed, and came near the sun.

The Sun did not burn him. A child, he thought, who knows no wrong; and a great work waits on him.

That same day, says Agastya, Rahu came to seize the sun.

On the sun’s own chariot the child touched him. Rahu, crusher of sun and moon, drew back in fright, and went straight to Indra’s hall, frowning with rage.

“Slayer of Bala and Vritra! You gave me the sun and moon to ease my hunger. Why give them to another? I came at the hour of the eclipse, and another Rahu had already seized the sun!”

Indra sprang up from his seat, his golden garland still about him. He mounted Airavata, king of elephants, tall as a peak of , four-tusked, golden bells ringing.

Rahu went ahead. Behind him the king of the gods rode out, to where the sun and the child were.

Rahu raced on, leaving Indra behind. The child saw him rushing in like a mountain peak. Another fruit! He let go of the sun and sprang.

Rahu, who is nothing but a head, wheeled and fled. “Indra! Indra!”

“Do not fear,” said Indra. “I will kill him.”

Then the child saw Airavata. A bigger fruit! He ran at the king of elephants, and for a moment his form blazed, terrible as Indra and Agni.

Indra, not greatly angry, loosed the thunderbolt from his hand. The child fell onto a mountain; as he fell, his left jaw broke.

Jambavan tells it another way: three hundred yojanas up, the sun’s blaze flung the boy back; he did not lose heart; and Indra, in anger, hurled the thunderbolt.

The Wind saw his son fall, and his anger turned on Indra. In Agastya’s telling, he drew himself in, took the child, and went into a cave.

Breath stopped. Every creature stood stiff as wood. No Veda was chanted, no offering made. The three worlds were like a hell.

Gods, gandharvas, asuras and men ran to Brahma, bellies swollen with breath that could not move. “You gave us the Wind as lord of our lives. Why has he shut us in?”

“Indra struck down his son today, on Rahu’s word,” said Brahma. “The Wind moves in every body, having none of his own. Without him a body is only wood. Come. We must go to him and win him back, or perish.”

In the cave, the child lay in his father’s lap, shining like the sun, like fire, like gold. Brahma looked, and was moved to pity.

The Wind, stricken by his son’s death, rose with the child and fell at Brahma’s feet. Brahma raised him and stroked the child. At that touch, like a crop given water, the child lived again.

And the Wind moved once more through every creature. The worlds breathed, glad as lotus pools freed from frost and heat.

“This child will do your work one day,” Brahma told the gods. “Give him boons, to please the Wind.”

Indra lifted a garland of lotuses. “My thunderbolt struck his hanu, his jaw. Let him be called Hanuman. My thunderbolt will never kill him.”

“A hundredth of my radiance,” said the Sun. “When he can study, I will teach him. None will match him in the shastras.” Varuna, Yama, Kubera, Shiva and Vishvakarma swore their weapons would never kill him.

“He will be unconquerable,” Brahma told the Wind. “For Ravana’s end and Rama’s joy, he will do deeds that make hair stand on end.”

Jambavan names two: Brahma’s, that no weapon would kill him in battle; Indra’s, death only when he chose.

Full of strength, Agastya says, the boy ran riot in the ’ hermitages, breaking ladles, scattering bark cloths. The sages cursed him. “You trouble us, trusting your strength. You will not know it for a long time. When someone reminds you of your glory, it will grow.”

On the shore, old Jambavan was doing just that.

“You are Kesari’s son and the Wind’s own, his equal in leaping. Our life-breath is all but gone. Rise, tiger among ! Cross the great ocean!”

And Hanuman grew.

The forgot their grief and roared his praise. Lashing his tail for joy, he stretched like a lion in a mountain cave; his face blazed like smokeless fire.

“I could chase the blazing sun from its rising,” he said, “reach it before it sets, and come back without touching the ground. I will see Sita! Rejoice, !”

He climbed Mahendra. Under his feet the great mountain was like an elephant a lion has struck, and it shook.

In his mind, he was already in Lanka.
