Two princes lie fallen in the night. An old bear asks one question, and Hanuman crosses the sea for herbs that vanish at his coming.

Hanuman and the mountain of herbs. Also known as: Sanjeevani booti, Hanuman lifts the mountain.

  • Courage

Night lay over the field before Lanka. Rama and Lakshmana did not stir. Indrajit’s weapon, the one Brahma had given him, had struck them down, and sixty-seven crores of monkeys had fainted around them. The leaders of the army could not tell what to do.

Vibhishana spoke. “Do not be afraid. The sons of Dasharatha bowed to Brahma’s weapon and let it strike them. This is no hour for despair.”

Hanuman had honoured the weapon too. “Let us comfort every one of them who still has life,” he said.

They took torches and walked out among the fallen, monkeys big as hills, flaming weapons scattered between them. Sugriva lay there, and Angada, Nila, Sushena, Nala. The torches moved on.

They were looking for one face. At last the torchlight found Jambavan, the old bear born of Brahma, marked by hundreds of arrows, like a fire going out.

Vibhishana bent to him. “Revered one, I hope the sharp arrows have not taken your life.”

Jambavan answered with difficulty. “Lord of the rakshasas, I know you by your voice. I cannot see you.” Then: “Tell me. Hanuman, Anjana’s son, the Wind’s son. Does he live?”

“You pass over Rama and Lakshmana,” said Vibhishana, “and ask for him? You show no such love to Sugriva, or Angada, or Rama.”

“Listen, then. If Hanuman lives, the whole army may lie dead and be unharmed. If he has given up his life, we are all dead though we breathe.”

Hanuman bent and took the old bear’s feet. At the sound of his voice, Jambavan felt as if he had been born again.

“Come,” said Jambavan. “Protect the lives of the monkeys. This is the hour for your strength. Heal Rama and Lakshmana. Go over the ocean to the Himalaya. You will see Rishabha there, the golden mountain, and the peak of . Between them stands a blazing mountain that holds every herb, and on its crown four herbs light the ten quarters.

“Mrita-sanjivani, which calls back the dead. Vishalyakarani, which takes out weapons and heals what they did. Suvarnakarani, which restores the body’s colour. Sandhani, which joins what was broken. Bring them, quickly.”

The words filled Hanuman with strength, like a swelling sea.

Hanuman stood on a mountain’s summit and pressed down with his feet. The peak could not bear him. It sank. Trees fell and took fire from the speed of him, and its summits broke. In Lanka the gates burst and houses shook, and the city seemed to dance in the night.

From there he climbed Mount Malaya and grew until he was like a cloud. He opened a mouth that shone like the fire under the sea and roared, and the rakshasas of Lanka could not stir.

He bowed to Rama. He raised his tail like a serpent, arched his back, laid back his ears, and sprang. His rush swept up trees, hills, a few monkeys, and dropped them in the water.

Below him the sea churned, and all its creatures whirled. He went like the discus loosed from Vishnu’s fingers, past hills and flocks of birds, lakes and rivers and crowded towns, along the sun’s own road, fast as the Wind, his father.

Then the Himalaya: waterfalls, caves, peaks like white clouds, the hermitages of . . And golden Rishabha, lit by blazing herbs.

He walked round it, searching. But the herbs knew that someone had come for them, and every one of them vanished from his sight.

Hanuman’s eyes went red as fire. “Prince of mountains,” he said, “have you no pity for Rama? Then be broken today by the strength of my arms.”

He seized the crest, with its thousands of minerals, its trees, its elephants, its gold, and sprang. Its edges shattered. The worlds were afraid, and the gods and their chiefs with them, and the beings of the air sang his praise. He flew with the speed of Garuda.

Up on the sun’s path, the shining peak in his hands, he blazed like a second sun beside the first. He looked like Vishnu holding his thousand-edged discus.

Below, the monkeys saw him and shouted. Hanuman roared back in joy. Lanka heard both, and the rakshasas answered with a roar more terrible still.

He came down among the army, bowed his head to the monkeys, and embraced Vibhishana.

The scent of the herbs went through the army. Rama breathed it, and Lakshmana, and their wounds were healed where they lay. The monkeys who had died stirred, free of pain, and rose as sleepers rise when night is ending.

One thing more Valmiki records here. Since the first day of the war, on Ravana’s orders, every rakshasa the monkeys killed had been thrown into the sea.

Hanuman took the mountain of herbs and carried it back to the Himalaya at once, and came again to Rama.

Later in the war Valmiki has Hanuman fetch herbs a second time. This time he cannot tell the herbs apart, and the peak is lifted for a different reason. And Lakshmana falls again.

Ravana’s horses lay dead, and he had leapt down from his chariot. He threw a spear at Vibhishana; Lakshmana cut it in three. He took up another, one that even Yama would not meet. Lakshmana ran to shield Vibhishana, arrows streaming.

“You saved him,” said Ravana. “Then this spear is for you.” It flew, hung with eight bells. “May it be well with Lakshmana!” Rama cried to it. “May you prove useless!”

It struck Lakshmana’s broad chest, and he fell.

Rama’s eyes filled. “What use to me is life, or the fight? If he is dead, I will follow him to Yama’s house. What shall I say to Sumitra? Wives are found anywhere, kin anywhere. A brother of the same womb, nowhere.”

Sushena spoke. “Give up this grief. Lakshmana’s face has not changed. It is bright. His palms are like lotus petals, his eyes clear. The dead do not look so. His heart beats; he sleeps.”

Then, to Hanuman: “Go quickly to Mount Oushadhi, the one Jambavan once described. On its southern peak grow the herbs.”

Hanuman reached it and could not tell the herbs apart. He thought of time slipping away. He shook the peak three times, tore it up, and sprang.

Sushena crushed the herb and gave it by the nose. Lakshmana rose, free of spear and pain.

“Come, come,” said Rama, and held him, eyes clouded. “By good fortune I see you back from death.”
