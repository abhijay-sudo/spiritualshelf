He knew how to break into Drona’s wheel, not how to break out. One boy against an army, until six warriors closed in.

Abhimanyu and the Chakravyuh. Also known as: Chakravyuha, Abhimanyu Vadh.

  • Courage
  • Fearlessness

The preceptor had formed the great circular array, and at its mouth stood ten thousand princes in red robes, sworn to stand by one another. The Pandavas charged it, and Drona hurled them back like a hill breaking a wave.

Arjuna was far away, fighting the Samsaptakas. Yudhishthira turned to his nephew, Subhadra’s son.

“Child, we do not know how to break this array. Only you, Arjuna, Krishna or Pradyumna can. No fifth man exists. Break it, or Arjuna will reproach us all when he returns.”

“My father taught me how to pierce it and strike within,” Abhimanyu said. “But if danger overtakes me, I shall not be able to come out.”

“Break it once and make a path,” said Yudhishthira. “We will follow your track and guard you on every side.”

“I will follow you myself,” said Bhima, “with Dhrishtadyumna, Satyaki and the Panchalas.”

“Then I will enter it,” said Abhimanyu, “like an insect in a rage entering a blazing fire.”

His charioteer Sumitra hesitated. “Heavy is the burden they have laid on you. Drona is a master of great weapons. You were raised in luxury and are new to battle.”

Abhimanyu laughed. “Who is this Drona? I would face Indra on Airavata. Go!”

The three-year-old steeds in golden trappings sprang forward under his karnikara banner. In Drona’s very sight, the boy broke the array and was inside.

They closed on him from every side, cars and elephants and horsemen, roaring “Wait! Come to me!” He shot so fast that they fell upon him like insects falling into fire. The Kaurava ranks broke and ran.

Duryodhana himself wheeled back to face him. “Rescue the king!” Drona cried, and the great car-warriors poured arrows around Duryodhana and snatched him away.

Abhimanyu would not bear that morsel taken from his mouth. He gave a lion’s roar. The son of the Asmaka king called “Wait!” and fell, the boy smiling all the while. One shaft went through Karna’s mail, and Karna shook like a hill in an earthquake. Shalya sank down fainting on his car.

The whole army fled like deer.

Drona watched with eyes wide in delight. “I know no bowman his equal,” he told Kripa. “If he wished, he could slay this whole host.”

Duryodhana heard, and smiled thinly. “The preceptor spares him because Arjuna is his disciple. Crush the boy!”

“I will devour him,” roared Dushasana, “as Rahu swallows the sun!”

Abhimanyu met him smiling. “So I see the man who taunted Yudhishthira in the hall and rode on Shakuni’s dice. Today I pay my debt to my father, to Bhima, to Draupadi.”

One shaft, then twenty-five more. Dushasana slumped in his car, and his charioteer bore him out of the fight. Then Karna came, and Abhimanyu cut down his bow and banner with a single arrow.

Behind him, the Pandavas rushed the breach he had torn. One man stood in their way.

Jayadratha, king of the Sindhus, had once been humbled by Bhima for his insult to Draupadi. He had starved his body till the veins stood out, and worshipped Mahadeva. In a dream the god asked what he wanted.

“Alone, on a single car, I will hold back all the sons of Pandu.”

“Except Arjuna,” said Shiva. “The other four you shall check.”

Now, under a silver boar on his banner, Jayadratha filled every gap the boy had made. Yudhishthira cut his bow; he took another. He brought down Bhima’s bow and horses. None could pass him.

Inside the wheel, Abhimanyu was alone.

Rukmaratha, Shalya’s son, rode at him. “What is Abhimanyu? I will take him alive!” He fell, and his friends, a hundred princes with bows six cubits long, buried the boy in arrows.

Then Abhimanyu loosed the Gandharva weapon his father had won by penance. He seemed now one, now a hundred, now a thousand. The hundred princes went down like young mango trees in a storm.

Duryodhana himself came at him and was driven back. Then came Lakshmana, Duryodhana’s own son, beautiful and fearless with inexperience. “Look well on this world,” Abhimanyu told him, “for soon you go to the next.”

Lakshmana fell. Duryodhana’s cry went through the host.

“Slay him!”

Six car-warriors closed around Abhimanyu.

“Let us all grind him together,” Shakuni urged, “or he will kill us one by one.”

“Abhimanyu grinds us all,” Karna said to Drona. “Tell us how to slay him.”

“Has any of you found the smallest gap in him?” Drona answered. “Only his bow, drawn to a circle, can be seen. Though he wounds me, he gladdens me. I see no difference between him and Arjuna.”

“I stay only because a warrior must,” said Karna. “His arrows burn my heart.”

Drona smiled. “I taught his father how armour is worn. His mail cannot be pierced. Cut his bow, reins and horses. Bow in hand, gods and Asuras together could not conquer him.”

Karna cut the bow. Kritavarma killed the horses. Kripa killed the charioteers.

Bowless and carless, the six still raining arrows on him, Abhimanyu took up sword and shield and sprang into the air. He wheeled across the sky like Garuda, and the great bowmen watched upward, fearing he would drop on them.

Drona’s arrow cut his jewelled sword at the hilt. Karna’s shafts split his shield.

He came down to earth unhurt, seized a chariot wheel and rushed at Drona. Bright with the dust of chariot wheels, the wheel lifted high, he looked like Krishna himself raising the discus.

The kings, in dread, shattered the wheel into a hundred pieces.

He took up a mace. Ashwatthama leapt from his car and took three long leaps out of its reach.

Mace in hand, Abhimanyu crushed horses, cars and elephants. Then Dushasana’s son came on foot, shouting “Wait!”, and the two cousins fought mace to mace. Each struck the other down. Dushasana’s son rose first, and struck Abhimanyu as he was rising. Spent with that blow and with the long day’s fight, the son of Subhadra fell and did not rise.

The Kauravas roared in triumph. Creatures of the sky cried aloud: “Six great car-warriors, led by Drona and Karna, have slain one who fought alone. This was not righteous.”

The Pandava warriors put off their armour, laid down their bows and sat around Yudhishthira, brooding on the boy. “How shall I look at Arjuna,” he said, “and at Subhadra, robbed of her son?”
