Draupadi’s swayamvara. Also known as: Draupadi swayamvar, Arjuna and the bow, The bow no king could string.

On the road from Ekachakra the five Pandavas and their mother met a company of brahmins. “Where are you going, and where have you come from?” the brahmins asked.

“We are brothers travelling with our mother,” said . “We come from Ekachakra.”

“Then go this very day to King Drupada’s house in Panchala,” they said. “A great svayamvara is to be held, where his daughter will choose her own husband. She rose from the middle of the sacrificial altar. Her eyes are like lotus petals, and her body smells of the blue lotus. Come and see the festival. You are all handsome as gods. Who knows? She may choose one of you.”

“We will go with you,” Yudhishthira replied, “to see this maiden’s svayamvara.”

So they turned towards the southern Panchalas, whom Drupada ruled. On the way they saw the Dwaipayana, Vyasa, saluted him, spoke with him, and went on at his command, by slow stages, resting beside lovely woods and lakes.

In the Panchala capital they took rooms in a potter’s house and lived on alms, as brahmins do. No one knew them.

King Drupada had long wished to give his daughter to , but he had told no one. Thinking of Arjuna, he had a very stiff bow made, one that no one but Arjuna could bend. Machinery was raised in the sky, with a mark fixed to it.

“Whoever strings this bow,” Drupada proclaimed, “and with these arrows shoots the mark above the machine, shall have my daughter.”

Kings came from many lands, Duryodhana and among them. The great amphitheatre stood north-east of the city, shaded by a many-coloured canopy and loud with trumpets. The Pandavas went in and sat among the brahmins.

Actors, dancers and gifts filled the festival until the sixteenth day. Then , washed and richly dressed, came in with a golden dish and a garland. The priest lit the fire, and the music stopped.

Holding his sister’s arm, Dhrishtadyumna called out: “Hear me, kings! This is the bow, that is the mark, and these are the arrows. Shoot through the opening of the machine with these five sharp arrows. He who does it wins my sister today.”

Tellings differ about the mark. Some make it a golden fish’s eye, aimed at by reflection. Ganguli says only “the mark.”

One by one the young kings stood up, each proud of his beauty, birth, strength and wealth. “She shall be mine!” each one said. Gods came in their chariots to watch from the sky. of the Yadavas and his brother sat with the Yadava chiefs, looking on.

Then the kings tried the bow. It was too stiff for them. Some were thrown to the ground by it and lay still a while, panting, crowns and garlands loosened, their hope of the maiden gone cold.

Shishupala, king of the Chedis, fell on his knees. Jarasandha stood before the bow like a mountain, fell to his knees as well, got up, and left the amphitheatre for his own kingdom. Shalya, king of Madra, fell on his knees too.

So the kings became a subject of mockery.

Then Karna, the foremost of bowmen, raised the bow, strung it, and set the arrows on the string. Shining like fire, he seemed about to shoot the mark. The sons of Pandu thought it as good as shot.

But Draupadi, seeing him, said in a loud voice, “I will not choose a Suta for my husband!”

Karna was the Sun’s son, Radha’s son, as the text has it; the text calls him of the Suta tribe. It gives her no reason and him no answer. Laughing in vexation, he looked at the Sun and threw aside the bow, already drawn to a circle.

Not every telling has this. In some northern manuscripts she refuses him for his birth; in the Critical Edition, Wikipedia says, she raises no objection and Karna simply fails to string the bow.

When no king was left to try, Arjuna rose from among the brahmins. As he went towards the bow, the brahmins shook their deerskins and clamoured. Some were displeased, some pleased.

“How can a brahmin boy untrained in arms string what Shalya and other great kshatriyas could not?” said some. “If he fails, the kings will laugh at every brahmin.”

“Look at his shoulders and arms, and his walk like a lion’s,” said others. “He would not come unless he could. Parashurama defeated the kshatriyas. Agastya drank the ocean. Let him string it.” Many said, “So be it.”

Arjuna stood like a mountain beside the bow, walked round it, bowed his head to the lord Ishana, and thought of Krishna. Then he lifted it, and strung it in the twinkling of an eye.

Arjuna took up the five arrows and shot the mark. It fell to the ground through the opening in the machine above.

A great shout rose in the sky and in the amphitheatre. The gods rained flowers on his head. Thousands of brahmins waved their upper cloths for joy. Musicians struck up together, and bards sang his praise. The kings who had failed cried out in sorrow and dismay. Drupada was filled with joy.

In the uproar Yudhishthira and the twins hastily left for their lodging. Draupadi, glad at the sight of Arjuna, who looked like Indra himself, came to him with a white robe and a garland. The brahmins saluted the winner. He left the arena, and she followed close behind him, his wife.

Drupada wished to give his daughter to the brahmin who had shot the mark. The other kings grew angry. “He passes us by as if we were straw!” they said. “The Vedic word is that a svayamvara is for kshatriyas. Brahmins have no claim.” But the brahmin must not be killed, they said: “our kingdoms and lives exist for brahmins.”

Drupada they meant to kill. They rushed at him with weapons, and he fled to the brahmins for shelter. and Arjuna stepped out against the kings. Bhima tore up a great tree and stood beside Arjuna like Yama with his mace.

Krishna saw. “That is Arjuna,” he told Balarama, “and that is Bhima. The one who left early is Yudhishthira. They escaped the house of lac!” Balarama was glad that and her sons were alive.

The brahmins shook their deerskins. “Fear not, we will fight the kings!” Arjuna smilingly told them to stand aside. He and Bhima stood firm.

“A brahmin who wants to fight may be fought,” said the kings. Karna rushed at Arjuna, and Shalya at Bhima. Karna and Arjuna fought until neither could be seen for arrows. Karna asked, “Are you Parashurama, or Indra, or Vishnu in a brahmin’s form?” Arjuna is a kshatriya, but he answered, “I am only a brahmin, taught the Brahma and Paurandara weapons by my teacher’s grace.” Karna thought a brahmin’s power cannot be beaten, and stopped.

Bhima lifted Shalya and threw him down, without hurting him much. “These brahmins are fine warriors,” said the kings. “Let us find out who they are.” Krishna said, “The maiden was justly won.” The kings went home, wondering.

Kunti waited anxiously; her sons were late from begging. They came back to the potter’s house and told her that Draupadi was the alms they had got that day. Kunti, in the room, did not see them. “Enjoy it all together,” she said.

Then she saw Draupadi. “Oh, what have I said!” Afraid of sin, she took Draupadi by the hand to Yudhishthira and asked how her words could stay true without any sin touching the princess.

Yudhishthira thought a moment and told Arjuna to wed her, since he had won her. Arjuna refused: Yudhishthira must wed first, then Bhima, then himself. The brothers looked at Draupadi, and she at them. Yudhishthira remembered Dwaipayana’s words and, fearing a split among the brothers, said, “Draupadi shall be the common wife of us all.” The text gives these reasons only.
