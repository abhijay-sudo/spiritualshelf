Every god is invited to Daksha’s great sacrifice but Shiva. Daksha’s daughter Sati goes anyway, and finds no share set out for her husband.

The sacrifice without Shiva. Also known as: Daksha Yagna, Sati Daha.

  • Dignity
  • Devotion
हिन्दी

Prayaga. The had gathered for a great sacrifice, and Shiva sat among them with Sati.

Then Daksha walked in, lord of the Prajapatis, shedding light as he came. The heavenly bowed to him with folded hands.

Shiva kept his seat, and did not bow.

Daksha’s eyes hardened. “Let every god and brahmana hear me,” he said. “This dweller in cremation grounds, ringed by ghosts, shall have no share with the gods in any sacrifice.”

Nandi, Shiva’s attendant, stared, eyes rolling with fury. “Fool! You curse him whose very thought makes sacrifices bear fruit? Before long you will wear the face of a goat.”

Shiva laughed. “Do not be angry, Nandi. I have not been cursed. I am the sacrifice.”

Daksha went home, seething.

At Kanakhala, Daksha began a great sacrifice.

Tvashtri raised shining mansions for the guests. Vishnu was brought from Vaikuntha, Brahma from Satyaloka. The guardians of the quarters stood armed at the gates. Bhrigu was a priest there, one of eighty-six thousand.

Shiva was not invited. Nor was Sati, though she was Daksha’s beloved daughter.

The Dadhichi spoke. “Where is Shiva? Without him this cannot be whole. Send for him, and for Sati.”

Daksha mocked him. “Vishnu is here, in whom all sacrifices rest. Brahma is here. What use is Shiva to us? I gave him my daughter only because Brahma pressed me.”

“Then this is no sacrifice,” said Dadhichi. “Your ruin is near, and in this very .”

He walked out.

On Mount Gandhamadana, Sati was at play among her friends when she saw the moon hurrying past with Rohini.

“Where is he going?” Her friend Vijaya went to ask, and came back shaken. “To Daksha’s sacrifice.”

Sati went to Shiva, who sat among his ganas. “My father holds a great sacrifice. Come with me.”

“He is your father,” said Shiva, “but he is my enemy. Whoever goes uninvited to another’s house meets a disrespect harder than death. An enemy’s arrows hurt less than the taunts of one’s own kin.”

“He left you out, you who make sacrifice bear fruit. I would know his mind. Give me leave.”

“Then go, in royal state, on this bull.”

Sixty thousand ganas went with her, singing.

At her father’s gate Sati got down from the bull and went in alone.

Her mother and her sisters welcomed her. Daksha gave no sign of love. The rest, afraid of him, did not greet her.

She bowed to her parents. Then she saw the shares: one for Vishnu, one for every god. None for Shiva.

Her gaze burned. “Can there be a sacrifice without Shiva? He is the sacrifice. Vishnu, Brahma, : how do you sit here without him?”

Vishnu, the gods and the kept silent.

Daksha looked at her coldly. “Why did you come at all? Stay or go. Your husband is lord of ghosts and knows no custom; I did not invite him. Now smile, and take your own share.”

Sati drew a hard breath.

“Who but you could hate Shiva, who hates no one? The two syllables of his name, spoken once, wipe out sin. Yes, he lives on the cremation ground with matted hair; and gods and wear the dust of his feet on their heads. He calls me Dakshayani, and the name pains me. This body was born of you. I want no more of it.”

She fell silent. She sat on the ground, to the north, sipped water, and covered herself with her cloth. Eyes shut, she drew her breath up to the point between her brows and thought only of Shiva’s feet.

By her own will, in the fire of yoga, she gave up her body.

“Alas! Alas!” The cry went over the earth and up into the sky.

At the door, Shiva’s ganas rose with their weapons and rushed at Daksha. Bhrigu poured an offering into the southern fire with a Yajus mantra, and the Ribhus sprang up in thousands, firebrands in their hands. The ganas were driven back.

Then a voice spoke out of the sky.

“Daksha, what have you done? You would not heed Dadhichi. Your own daughter came to your house and you did not honour her. Shiva is the father of the universe, Sati its mother. This sacrifice will be destroyed. Gods, , leave this altar. Vishnu, Brahma, come away.”

No one moved. No one spoke.

The ganas who were left fled to Shiva.

Shiva called Narada to mind, and Narada came and told him all.

Shiva tore a cluster of matted hair from his head and struck it on the mountain top. It split in two, with a sound like the end of the worlds.

From one half rose Virabhadra, tall as the sky, blazing, with arms in thousands. From the other, Mahakali, among crores of spirits.

Virabhadra folded his hands. “Command me, Lord. Shall I dry up the oceans in half a moment? Grind the mountains to dust? Without your word not a blade of grass moves. Send me.”

“Be victorious,” said Shiva. “Daksha is holding a sacrifice. Destroy it, with all that belongs to it, and come back quickly.”

Ten thousand lions drew the chariots.

At Kanakhala the earth shook, and Daksha saw stars at noon.

A roar came, and the quarters went dark. Daksha fell flat at Vishnu’s feet. “Protect my sacrifice.”

“Who can shield you from Rudra’s wrath?” said Vishnu. “This is beyond my power to stop.”

The gods fought all the same, Vishnu among them. His discus stood still in his hand. His bow broke in three. He and Brahma withdrew.

The sacrifice itself took the shape of a deer and fled up the sky. Virabhadra caught it.

Daksha hid behind the altar. Virabhadra dragged him out. No weapon could cut him. With his hand he took Daksha’s head and cast it into the fire.

On , Shiva made Virabhadra chief of his ganas.

The beaten gods went to Brahma, and with him to Vishnu. “Fall at his feet,” said Vishnu. “He has lost his beloved.”

On , beyond Kubera’s city, stood a banyan a hundred yojanas high. Under it sat Shiva, ash-smeared, calm, teaching Narada. He rose, and bowed to Vishnu.

The gods asked pardon. Shiva laughed. “I do not count my children’s faults. Let Daksha have a goat’s head.”

At Kanakhala the sacrificial goat’s head was joined to the body. Shiva looked, and Daksha woke as if from sleep, his mind clear as the autumn moon. “You have blessed me,” he said, “in the guise of punishing me.”

The sacrifice was completed, with Shiva’s full share.

And Sati was born again, daughter of Mena and Himavan.

  1. sage ऋषि ṛṣi

    Literal“Seer.” One who perceives; the Vedic word for a poet-visionary who hears rather than invents.

    MeaningA rishi: a seer, teacher or ascetic living in the forest.

  2. rite यज्ञ yajña

    LiteralFrom yaj, to worship, to offer. The fire-rite of the Vedas; later, any disciplined act given away.

    MeaningA sacrifice: offerings made into a sacred fire with mantras, for a god or for a purpose; also any act performed as an offering.

  3. Kailasa कैलास kailāsa

    MeaningThe Himalayan mountain that is Shiva’s home.
