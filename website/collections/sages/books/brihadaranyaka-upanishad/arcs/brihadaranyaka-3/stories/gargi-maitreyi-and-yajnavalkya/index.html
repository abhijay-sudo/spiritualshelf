A king offers a thousand cows, gold tied to their horns, to the wisest. Gargi rises with two questions against the man who had them driven off; at home, Maitreyi asks him one.

Gargi, Maitreyi and Yajnavalkya. Also known as: Gargi Vachaknavi questions Yajnavalkya, Yajnavalkya and Maitreyi.

King Janaka of Videha was holding a sacrifice of rich gifts, and the brahmanas of the Kurus and the Panchalas had crowded in for it. One question stirred in the king: which of them was the most learned?

So he penned in a thousand cows. To the horns of each were tied ten padas of gold.

“Venerable brahmanas,” said Janaka, “let the one among you who best knows Brahman drive these cows away.”

No one dared.

Then Yajnavalkya turned to his own student. “Drive them off, my dear.”

And the student drove them off.

A thousand cows went out, gold on their horns. And the brahmanas burned with anger against Yajnavalkya.

“How can he call himself the best among us?”

Ashvala, Janaka’s own hotri priest, put it to his face. “Yajnavalkya, are you really the best knower of Brahman here?”

“I bow to the best knower of Brahman,” said Yajnavalkya. “But I do want these cows.”

So the questioning began. Ashvala asked how one who offers the sacrifice gets free of death’s grasp. He was answered, and held his peace. Artabhaga came next, then Bhujyu, then Ushasta, then Kahola. Each asked. Each was answered. Each held his peace.

Then a woman spoke.

“Yajnavalkya,” said Gargi Vachaknavi, “everything here is woven in water, warp and weft. In what, then, is water woven?”

“In air, Gargi.”

“And air?”

“In the worlds of the sky, Gargi.”

She did not stop, and neither did he. The worlds of the sky were woven in the worlds of the Gandharvas; those, in the worlds of the sun; those, in the moon’s; the moon’s, in the stars’; the stars’, in the worlds of the gods; the gods’, in Indra’s; Indra’s, in Prajapati’s; Prajapati’s, in the worlds of Brahman.

“And the worlds of Brahman? In what are they woven, warp and weft?”

“Gargi, do not ask too much, or your head will fall off. You are asking too much about a deity one must not ask too much about. Gargi, do not ask too much.”

Gargi Vachaknavi held her peace. For now.

Uddalaka Aruni questioned him next, and turned the same warning on Yajnavalkya: drive those cows away without knowing the thread that strings this world and the next, and the ruler within, and his head would fall off. Yajnavalkya named the thread, air, and the immortal ruler within. Uddalaka held his peace.

Then Vachaknavi stood again.

“Venerable brahmanas, I will ask him two questions. If he answers them, none of you will ever beat him in talk of Brahman.”

“Ask, Gargi.”

“As a warrior’s son of Kashi or Videha strings his slackened bow, takes two foe-piercing arrows in his hand and rises to fight, so I rise against you, Yajnavalkya, with two questions. Answer them.”

“Ask, Gargi.”

“That which they say is above the sky and beneath the earth, which is between sky and earth, which they call past, present and to come: in what is it woven, warp and weft?”

“In space, Gargi.”

“I bow to you, Yajnavalkya. Make ready for the second.”

She asked the same thing again, and again he said: in space.

“And space? In what is space woven?”

This time Yajnavalkya did not warn her off.

“Gargi, the brahmanas call it the Akshara, the Imperishable. Not coarse, not fine, not short, not long. Without shadow, without darkness, without taste or smell, without eyes, ears, speech or mind. It has no inside and no outside. It eats nothing, and nothing eats it.”

“At the command of this Imperishable, Gargi, sun and moon stand apart. Heaven and earth stand apart. Moments, hours, days and nights, months, seasons and years stand apart. Some rivers flow east from the white mountains, others west.

“Whoever offers, sacrifices and does penance for many thousands of years without knowing it, his work comes to an end. It is unseen, yet it sees; unheard, yet it hears. In this Imperishable, Gargi, space is woven, warp and weft.”

Gargi turned to the assembly.

“Venerable brahmanas, count it a great thing if you get away from him with a bow. None of you will ever beat him in talk of Brahman.”

Then Vachaknavi held her peace.

Elsewhere in the same Upanishad, Yajnavalkya is at home. He has two wives, Maitreyi and Katyayani, and of the two it is Maitreyi who speaks of Brahman.

And now he is about to go forth.

“Maitreyi, I am going away from this place. Let me make a settlement between you and Katyayani.”

“My lord, if this whole earth, full of wealth, were mine, would I be immortal by it?”

“No. Your life would be like the life of the rich. But there is no hope of immortality through wealth.”

“Then what should I do with what will not make me immortal? What you know, my lord, tell me that.”

“You are dear to me, and you speak dear words. Come, sit. I will explain.”

“A husband is dear, not for the husband’s sake, but for the Self. A wife, sons, wealth, the gods, everything: dear for the Self. The Self must be seen, heard, pondered and meditated on, Maitreyi.

“As a lump of salt thrown into water dissolves and cannot be taken out, yet wherever you taste, it is salt, so this great Being rises from these elements and vanishes into them. Once departed, there is no more knowing.”

“Here you have bewildered me, sir.”

“I say nothing bewildering. Where there is as it were a two, one sees another. But when all has become the Self, by what should one see whom? By what should one know the knower? Thus far goes immortality.”

And Yajnavalkya went away.
