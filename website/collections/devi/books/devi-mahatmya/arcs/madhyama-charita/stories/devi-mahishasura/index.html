The fury of every god becomes one Goddess. Against her stands Mahishasura, who seized heaven and changes shape as he fights.

The Goddess and Mahishasura. Also known as: Mahishasura Mardini (the traditional name of the Goddess for this deed), Mahishasura Vadh.

  • Courage
  • Fearlessness

The army of the gods broke. For a full hundred years they had fought the asuras, Indra leading the gods, Mahisha the asuras. Now Mahishasura had conquered every one of them, and made himself Indra.

The beaten gods set Brahma, lord of creatures, at their head and went to where Shiva and Vishnu dwelt.

“He rules the realms of the Sun and Indra, of Agni, Vayu and the Moon, of Yama and Varuna, and of the other gods, himself. Cast out of heaven by that evil-souled Mahisha, the hosts of the gods wander the earth like mortals. We have come to you for refuge. Devise his death!”

Vishnu heard. Shiva heard. Both their faces darkened into frowns.

Out of Vishnu’s face, full of fury, burst a great light. It burst from Brahma’s face, and from Shiva’s. From the bodies of Indra and the other gods it poured forth, and it all ran together into one.

The gods saw a mass of radiance like a burning mountain, its flames filling every quarter of the sky. That light, born of every god’s body, gathered and became a woman, and the three worlds shone with her.

Shiva’s light became her face. Yama’s became her hair, Vishnu’s her arms. Brahma’s light shaped her feet, the Sun’s her toes. Agni’s gave her three eyes; Vayu’s, her ears.

The gods Mahisha had crushed looked upon her, and were glad. Then, one by one, they armed her.

Shiva drew a trident out of his own trident for her. Vishnu drew a discus out of his discus. Varuna gave a conch, and a noose. Agni gave a spear; Vayu, a bow and two quivers full of arrows. Indra drew a thunderbolt from his own thunderbolt, and the bell of his elephant Airavata. Yama gave a staff from his staff of death. Brahma gave a water-pot; Kala, a sword and a spotless shield. The Sun set his own rays in every pore of her skin.

Vishvakarma gave an axe, ornaments, and armour no weapon could pierce. The ocean gave unfading lotuses. Kubera, a cup brimming with wine. Shesha, who bears the earth, gave a necklace of serpents.

And Himavat gave her a lion.

Honoured with these gifts, the Goddess roared, and laughed aloud, again and again.

The sky filled with that sound. Its echo rolled on and on. The worlds shook, the seas trembled, the earth quaked, the mountains moved. “Conquer!” the gods cried with joy to her who rode the lion, and the bowed in praise.

The enemies of the gods saw the three worlds in turmoil and rose as one, weapons raised.

“Ha! What is this?” roared Mahishasura in his wrath, and rushed at the sound, all his asuras around him.

Then he saw her. Her light filled the three worlds. The earth bowed beneath her tread; her crown grazed the sky. Her bowstring’s twang shook the underworld. Her thousand arms reached every horizon.

The battle began, and every quarter of the sky blazed with flying weapons.

Chikshura, Mahisha’s general, fought her, and Chamara with his army. Udagra came with sixty thousand chariots. Mahahanu fought with ten million, Asiloma with fifty million, Bashkala with six million. Ugradarshana brought ten million chariots and elephants and horses in thousands. Amid chariots, elephants and horses beyond all counting stood Mahishasura himself.

Maces, javelins, axes, nooses: all flew at one Goddess. She cut them apart as if at play, raining her own weapons back. Her face showed no strain.

Her lion, mane bristling, stalked through the asura ranks like fire through a forest. And every breath she drew in battle became a warrior: by hundreds and thousands, beating drums, blowing conchs.

Seeing his army fall, Chikshura charged in fury. His arrows poured on her as a cloud pours rain on Meru’s peak. She cut them away in play, shot his horses and his charioteer, split his bow and his high banner.

On foot, with sword and shield, he struck the lion on the head, then the Goddess, on her left arm. The sword shattered against it. Red-eyed, he hurled a blazing pike. Her own pike broke it into a hundred pieces, and him with it.

Chamara came on an elephant. His spear she struck down with a scornful hum. Then the lion leapt onto the elephant’s brow. Locked together, they fell. The lion sprang skyward, came down, and one blow of his paw ended Chamara.

The rest went down one by one: Udagra, Karala, Uddhata, Bashkala, Tamra and Andhaka, Ugrasya, Ugravirya, Mahahanu, Bidala, Durdhara, Durmukha.

Then Mahishasura took his own shape: the buffalo. Her troops he scattered with muzzle and hoof, with tail and horn, with the blast of his breath. Then he charged at her lion.

His hooves pounded the earth; his whirling rush crumbled it. His horns flung mountains into the air. His lashing tail drove the sea over its shores. His swinging horns tore the clouds apart, and the wind of his breath brought mountains crashing down from the sky by hundreds.

Chandika let her anger rise. She cast her noose over him and bound him fast.

He left the buffalo. He was a lion.

As she moved to strike the lion, he became a man, sword in hand. Her arrows pierced him, sword, shield and all. He became a huge elephant, dragged at her lion with his trunk, trumpeting. Her sword cut the trunk away.

The great asura was a buffalo again; the three worlds shook.

Chandika, mother of the world, drank from her cup again and again. She laughed, and her eyes glowed red.

Mahishasura bellowed, frenzied with his own might, and flung mountains at her with his horns. She ground them to dust with arrows, and spoke, words thick, mouth reddened with mead.

“Roar, fool, roar for your little moment while I drink. When I slay you here, the gods will soon be roaring.”

She sprang up and came down upon the great asura, pressed her foot on his neck, and struck him with her spear. Pinned beneath her foot, he came half out of his own mouth. Even half emerged, he fought on. With her great sword the Goddess struck him down.

The asura host perished, wailing. Gandharvas sang; apsaras danced. Indra and the gods bowed low, hair on end with joy. “Who can describe your form, or your valour? You are Durga, the boat across the hard sea of existence. Guard us with your spear and sword, your bell, the twang of your bow.”

“Choose a boon,” she said.

“Whenever we call you to mind, destroy our direst calamities.”

“So be it.” And Bhadrakali vanished.
