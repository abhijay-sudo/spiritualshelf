Murugan and the Vel. Also known as: Soorasamharam, Skanda Shashti story.

In Hindi: मुरुगन और वेल, सूरसंहारम, स्कंद षष्ठी की कथा.

Ten thousand years the sacrifice burned in the north. , son of Kashyapa and Maya, tended it with his brothers, lion-faced Simhamukha and elephant-faced Taraka. did not come.

So Surapadman threw himself into the fire.

A brahmin leaning on a staff came to the fire-pit and called down the Ganga. Out of the flames Surapadman rose roaring, like poison rising from the sea. Then Shiva shone in the sky on his great bull, the Goddess as half his body.

“Ask.”

“Every world. A body that never dies.”

Shiva gave him a thousand and eight universes for a hundred and eight ages, a body hard as vajra, armies like the sea. Then he told the brothers, “Who can conquer you? Only our own Shakti.”

Gold walls rose from the southern sea: Veeramahendram, built for him by the gods’ own craftsman. Brahma crowned him. The sun and moon held his parasol.

The king gave his orders. To the sun: “Do not pass over my walls, and keep your rays mild.” To the moon: “No more waxing and waning. Come to my city full.”

To and the gods: “You are the asuras’ younger brothers. Bring them fish from the sea, every day.”

Varuna heaped the catch on the shore, and the gods carried it into the city on their heads. “Better to die,” they wept. The asuras jeered: “Have they no shame?”

Indra slipped away to earth with his queen. Heaven went dark, like a night without moon or stars.

Then Shiva gave the gods a son: six sparks leapt from the eyes on his six foreheads and in the Saravana pool became a six-faced child, Murugan, also called .

Indra bowed to the boy. “Kill Surapadman, then rule heaven yourself. We will serve you.”

Murugan smiled. “The old glory you offer us, we give back. You be the army; we will be its commander.” The gods bathed him in heavenly waters and hailed him commander.

Days went by. Indra came to with Brahma and Vishnu, waited long at the gate, then fell before Shiva.

“My son and the gods lie in his prison in the sea. We are like a crow on a ship out at sea: nowhere to land but you.”

Shiva turned to his son. “Destroy Surapadman and his hosts, who wrecked the gods’ city and torment every living thing. Set the path of the Vedas back in place. Give Indra his kingdom. Then come home.”

He made the eleven Rudras into weapons for the boy’s twelve hands. Then he gave him one blazing Vel, lord of every weapon, that could undo the elements of a universe and strip even gods of their boons.

drew her son close and kissed his head. “Destroy the asuras, and come back.”

At Sikkal, temple tradition tells it another way: there his mother Parvati herself gives him the Vel.

Vayu took the reins, and the gods shouted, “Surapadman is as good as dead!”

At Krauncha mountain, elephant-faced Taraka fought with illusions. Murugan let the Vel go. It went through Taraka and the mountain, bathed in the heavenly Ganga, and came back to his hand. Taraka fell.

From his camp at Tiruchendur, Murugan sent a messenger first: Veerabahu, one of his hundred thousand and nine. Veerabahu leapt the sea and walked into Surapadman’s court. To stand below the asura would shame his lord. He thought of Murugan, and a jewelled throne, bright as countless suns, came to him.

“Free the gods, or the Vel comes.”

Surapadman laughed. “The Vel? From a child whose milk-teeth have not grown! Let the sea swallow the world, I will not free them.”

Veerabahu rose. His throne lifted into the sky and was gone.

In council, thousand-headed Simhamukha pleaded with his brother. “Free the gods, and Lord Kumara will go home to before an eye can blink.”

Surapadman would not. So Simhamukha bowed to the brother who was a father to him. “Forgive me. Give me leave to go to war.”

Banugopan, Surapadman’s son, fell to Veerabahu’s sword. Simhamukha fought Murugan himself. His heads, struck down, grew back again and again, until one roar from Murugan stopped them. Then Murugan hurled the thunderbolt, and Simhamukha died.

The news reached Veeramahendram and threw the king to the ground.

“When Taraka died, I lost one arm. Today, lion-face, I have lost the other.”

Then Surapadman rode out himself.

Agni became a rooster on Murugan’s banner. Indra became a peacock, green as an emerald mountain. “Ride on me.” Murugan rode him across the sky.

And Murugan showed Surapadman his true form: earth, seas, gods, every universe within his body, shining like endless suns risen together.

Surapadman stood amazed. “I took him for a child. He is the root of everything. The messenger spoke the truth. My anger is gone; my heart melts like wax. My feet want to circle him, my hands to fold, my head to bow. Only pride holds me back. I have fought the Lord face to face. That fame will stand forever. Will this body?”

Murugan put the vision away, and Surapadman’s old mind returned.

Surapadman became a darkness that covered earth and sky, then a giant of many heads and countless hands, rising to devour the gods. They fled, crying out to Murugan.

Murugan looked at the Vel. “Split him. Go, in the blink of an eye.”

The Vel blazed like a thousand crore suns, and the darkness fell away. Surapadman smiled. “What can it do to me?” He plunged into the sea and rose as a mango tree: fire for garlands, smoke for leaves, golden blossoms, fruit like rubies, roots down to the tortoise that bears the earth. He shook, and the worlds cracked.

The Vel cut the great trunk. He fell, rose in his own form, drew his sword and roared. The Vel split him in two.

The Vel bathed in the heavenly Ganga and returned to Murugan’s hand.

But Shiva’s old boon would not let Surapadman perish. His two halves rose as a rooster and a peacock, like a jewelled mountain and an emerald mountain grown wings, and came roaring at the gods’ commander.

Murugan looked at them with grace, and their rage left them, as black iron turns gold.

“Be my banner,” he told the rooster. “Cry from my chariot.” It crowed until the worlds shook.

He stepped down from Indra onto the peacock. “Carry us.”

Indra and Agni took back their own forms. The gods went free, and at Tirupparankundram Indra gave Murugan his daughter Deivayanai.

Each Skanda Shashti, temple tradition re-enacts this battle at Tiruchendur.
